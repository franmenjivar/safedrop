The honest starting point

Delivery drones already handle emergencies well

Wing publishes designated landing zones and proactive contingency landings. Zipline flies redundant safety systems and a ballistic parachute. The FAA specifies lost-link procedures, return-to-launch, hold, and land-at-a-designated-waypoint. Remote pilots supervise fleets in real time.

Any pitch that opens with “drones can’t handle emergencies” is wrong, and a judge who knows the industry will stop listening. SafeDrop assumes all of that already works.

What already exists

Deterministic failsafes. Return-to-home. Preferred recovery zones. Obstacle avoidance. Traffic awareness. Remote pilot in command. SafeDrop replaces none of it.

What doesn’t

A recovery site chosen at mission-planning time, re-checked against what is standing on it this minute — and a route to it re-verified against traffic that took off after you launched.

The gap

A landing zone is a claim about right now

The preferred site was empty when the mission was planned. Since then: a delivery van parked on it, a school let out, a food truck arrived, a crew put cones down. None of that is in the map.

In Scenario 17 the largest, flattest, best-by-geometry site in central Austin is a real off-leash dog park. The polygon says leisure=park. The ground says six loose animals.

The claim SafeDrop actually makes

An agentic workflow can synthesise aircraft state, current ground conditions, candidate sites, and traffic into a verified, explainable contingency recommendation — better than a deterministic baseline, without letting a language model near the flight controls.

Architecture

Agents interpret. Tools calculate. A gateway decides what ships.

Every physical quantity in a SafeDrop recommendation comes from a deterministic tool. There is no tool that lets an agent assert a number, and three separate mechanisms enforce it.

DET

Compress the telemetry before an agent sees it

Raw rows never reach a model. A preprocessor turns a 20-second window into labelled state: battery CRITICAL, motor DEGRADED, GPS scatter in metres RMS. This is the project’s central bet, and it is measurable — the harness can re-run the same agents on raw CSV for comparison.

01

Which contingency branch?

Continue, return to base, divert, land immediately, or escalate. Agent 1 cannot estimate range — it calls feasibility tools for every option it is seriously considering and picks the least disruptive one the evidence supports.

tools: energy · endurance · destination / base / hub feasibility · reachability envelope

02

Which reachable site is suitable right now?

The agentic core. Open geometry is not current safety. Agent 2 gathers the ground feed, inspects current site imagery, checks data freshness — then calls a deterministic constraint checker whose verdict it cannot overrule.

tools: ground feed · vision · Austin GIS · OSM · weather · freshness · hard constraints

03

Can the aircraft actually get there?

Six checks per route: energy, obstacles, restricted airspace, UAV separation, descent corridor. The decision that matters is what a failure means — a separation conflict means replan the path; an energy shortfall means abandon this site. Getting that distinction right is the agent’s contribution.

tools: route planner · route energy · obstacles · restrictions · separation · descent corridor

DET

Nothing ships unverified

The gateway re-reads the stored tool results and requires every applicable check to be explicitly true. A check that never ran is not a pass. The operator briefing is then templated from the verified package — not written by a fourth agent — so no sentence can outrun its evidence.

Measured

Against a baseline that isn’t a straw man

The baseline is a real deterministic system: predefined thresholds, deterministic reachability, a preconfigured recovery list, nearest-feasible selection, static-map geofencing and obstacle avoidance. It is missing exactly what is being tested.

Both systems are scored by the same evaluator against ground truth authored before any agent ran — independently of either system’s own gateway, so neither can score well by declining to check anything.

Why this table is not a 100% claim

Fifteen scenarios is a small sample and the interval matters more than the point estimate. A perfect score on 15 is consistent with a true rate as low as 80%; on the original six it was as low as 61%. The two abstention cases, taken alone, are consistent with anything above 34%.

The scenarios, the ground truth, and the baseline’s configuration are all ours. An independent scenario author would be the single highest-value change to this evaluation. evaluation/analysis.py prints the intervals, the per-family breakdown, and seven named threats to validity.

Both systems already pick the right branch every time. The entire gain is in which site gets chosen and whether the route to it was verified — which is what the architecture predicted, and the honest framing: SafeDrop is not better at deciding that the aircraft must land. Repeated over 4 runs and 24 scenario executions, the outcome metrics did not move.

What went wrong

Every top-line metric read 100% while things were broken

Four defects, none caught by a failing test. Three were found by reading agent trajectories; the fourth by opening a map.

Parallel tool calls beat the prompt

Gemini issued a candidate’s four evidence tools at once, so the constraint checker ran before the imagery arrived and returned UNVERIFIED for everything — including a car park with five vehicles. Reconciliation silently carried the whole result. Fixed by making the tool refuse to answer while evidence is outstanding.

A false-safe hiding one position away

Vision reported 0 animals, at 0.9 confidence, on a pasture with four cattle. With no ground feed, that made a hazardous site VALID. The right site was still chosen — because it happened to be tried first. Last-resort sites now require corroboration from two independent sources.

Coordinates that were never real

Sites were placed by bearing and distance from an arbitrary aircraft position, so a “recovery yard” sat in front of a house. Nothing in the pipeline could catch it: every check was internally consistent. Synthetic geography is invisible to synthetic tests.

A metric that rewarded the wrong thing

Correct-abstention rate was computed over scenarios that did abstain rather than those that should. A system that abstained on everything would have scored 100% on it. Nothing failed; the number was simply measuring the wrong denominator.

A polygon is not open ground

OSM park boundaries enclose houses, service roads and playscapes. Each anchor is now placed at the polygon’s pole of inaccessibility and reverse-geocoded to measure the distance to the nearest mapped structure. 23 of 90 candidate sites failed and were dropped.

The hot take

Safety-critical agents should reason over verified operational state, not raw physical telemetry — and the corollary matters more: a system that only reports outcomes will report good outcomes right up until the backstop is the only thing still working.

Trajectory logging is not a debugging convenience. It is the only place these four failures were visible.

Boundaries

What this system will not do

It sends no commands

flight_command_sent is False on every code path. Recommendations go to a remote Pilot in Command, who flies them. The capability to command an aircraft does not exist in the code.

Nothing here is certified

Generated sites are labelled NON_CERTIFIED and require operator review. Thresholds are prototype experiment parameters, not regulatory standards.

Stale evidence never passes

Data past its freshness threshold yields UNVERIFIED at best. A traffic service that is down is not an empty sky — it is an escalation.

Abstention is a valid outcome

No reachable site, no ground-safe site, no verified route: the system stops and says so, rather than producing a fluent recommendation nothing supports.
